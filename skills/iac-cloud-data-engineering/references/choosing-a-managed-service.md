# Choosing a Managed Service

Most "which service should we use?" conversations are six separate questions asked at once, which is why they end in preference rather than a decision. Separate them and each has an answer you can source. Ask them together and the loudest attribute — usually the label on the product page — decides.

[`statefulness-and-the-one-way-door.md`](statefulness-and-the-one-way-door.md) supplies the ordering principle. Picking a compute engine sits on the top rung of the reversibility ladder: compute holds nothing between runs, so a wrong choice costs a re-run. But one of the six axes below — coupling — reaches the bottom rungs, because the API your producers, consumers and access policies bind to is not compute, and unbinding it is a program.

## The six axes

Name them and keep the names — `platform-archetypes.md` applies these same six to whole platform stacks.

- **Operational burden.** Who gets paged, for what, and what they can do once paged. Not "how much work" — *whose* work, and where it goes when it stops being yours.
- **Scaling granularity.** The smallest unit of capacity you can add or remove, in the vendor's own term — and whether the vendor exposes one at all. Always state the unit together with the mode or tier that makes it visible; the unit belongs to the mode, not the product.
- **Coupling.** What your code and your consumers bind to: an open protocol with more than one implementation, or an API only one vendor sells. This axis decides the price of leaving.
- **Throughput shape.** Steady, spiky, or predictably bursty, and what fraction of the day the load is near its peak. Decides provisioned versus on-demand, and it is a fact about your workload, not the product's.
- **Failure and recovery model.** What breaks, what the service still guarantees while broken, what it guarantees about ordering and delivery afterwards, and what you can do while waiting.
- **Ecosystem fit.** What already speaks to this thing: client libraries, connectors, the identity model, the tools your team runs.

A service that wins on five and loses on coupling has not won. Weight the axes by the reversibility of what they touch.

## Managed is a transfer of work, not a saving

The pitch for a managed service is that it removes operational work. It does not. It moves it — off your on-call rota and onto your bill and a support ticket queue. That reframing is this skill's, not any vendor's, and it is the most useful correction to make before comparing anything, because it turns "managed is less work" into a question with an answer: *less work for whom, and at what price when it goes wrong?*

Three things you give up, sourced rather than folklore:

- **Version pinning.** Databricks documents its serverless compute as "a versionless product, which means that Databricks automatically upgrades the serverless compute runtime to support enhancements and upgrades to the platform." A job whose output you must reproduce next quarter runs on a runtime whose version you do not control. That is Databricks' documented behaviour for that product; check any other serverless offering rather than generalising it.
- **Tuning knobs, and sometimes the security model with them.** AWS documents that "MSK Serverless requires IAM access control for all clusters. Apache Kafka access control lists (ACLs) are not supported." That is not only operational — it retires an authorization mechanism your existing Kafka tooling may depend on.
- **The ability to fix the incident yourself.** When a self-managed cluster misbehaves you read its logs, change its configuration and restart it. When a managed one misbehaves, your action is to open a ticket and relay an ETA you did not set. Often a good trade — only if made deliberately.

The honesty in the other direction: self-managed is not cheaper, it bills as headcount and attention instead of as an invoice line. Neither column is free; they are denominated differently.

## "Serverless" is a claim about operation, not about billing

The generalisation to kill on sight: *serverless means you pay per request*. It is false as a rule, and the counterexample is one line. Amazon documents that Redshift Serverless bills "in RPU hours on a per-second basis" against "the capacity used in a given duration" — capacity multiplied by time, with the word "query" appearing only to say when the clock runs. Two queries of equal duration on different capacity cost differently. That is not per-request billing; it is the provisioned shape with an automatic on/off switch.

Read the rest of the label the same way. Google's BigQuery pricing documentation defines the term by what you do not do — "You don't need to provision individual instances or virtual machines" — then describes two pricing models under it. AWS calls Glue "a serverless data integration service" and in the same documentation set requires you to pick a worker type, with capacity in units it calls DPUs and M-DPUs. AWS presents EMR Serverless as "a deployment option for Amazon EMR", and it still exposes configurable units called workers plus a "pre-initialized capacity" feature keeping a warm pool ready. Athena is closest to the popular story, and even there AWS's term is precise: "per-query billing, based on data scanned", sold alongside capacity reservations. Microsoft's Fabric Spark compute documentation does not use the word at all — it says "fully managed", and sizes work in capacity units and Spark vCores.

So: **the product name is never the answer, the billing unit is.** Ask what meter turns and what turns it — bytes scanned, capacity held per unit of time, requests, throughput delivered. Then ask the throughput-shape question, which decides:

- **Load close to its peak most of the time** favours a provisioned shape. You pay for the capacity either way; buying it as a standing unit is usually cheaper and gives you a knob to turn.
- **Load that is spiky, unpredictable, or idle most of the day** favours a consumption shape, since the alternative is paying for a peak you touch briefly.

Two traps sit on top of that. First, an on-demand mode hides the underlying mechanism rather than deleting it — AWS documents that Kinesis Data Streams in on-demand mode still distributes by partition key across shards it manages for you, and that it "does not detect and isolate hash keys that are driving a higher portion of incoming traffic to a particular shard", recommending provisioned mode for highly uneven keys. A skewed key is still a skewed key. Second, warm-capacity features such as EMR Serverless' pre-initialized capacity reintroduce a standing charge in exchange for start-up latency, moving the product back toward the provisioned shape you thought you had left. Billing shapes crossed against workload shapes are `sizing-and-the-cost-model.md`; here, the shape rather than the name is the selection input.

## The worked comparison: streaming ingest

This is the comparison [`the-log-and-partitioning.md`](../../streaming-data-engineering/references/the-log-and-partitioning.md) hands over. That file owns the log's semantics — partitions as the double atom of ordering and parallelism, key choice, `acks` and the in-sync replica set, retention versus compaction, replayability. None of that is re-taught here, and none of it changes because you bought the log from someone. The six axes are what changes. Verified 2026-08-08.

| Axis | Self-managed Apache Kafka | Amazon MSK | Kinesis Data Streams | Azure Event Hubs | Google Cloud Pub/Sub |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Operational burden** | Entirely yours: brokers, storage, upgrades, rebalancing, on-call | AWS runs the control plane; MSK Provisioned still asks you for broker count and type, and in MSK Serverless "MSK manages the broker nodes used to handle your traffic" | No cluster to run; you choose a capacity mode | Microsoft states "no servers, disks, or networks to manage and monitor and no brokers to consider or configure, ever" | Google calls it "wholly managed, serverless, and globally distributed" |
| **Scaling granularity** | Yours to size, at the broker and partition level | *Broker nodes* plus a broker type, in MSK Provisioned; MSK Serverless exposes no broker unit | *Shard*, in provisioned mode only; the on-demand modes expose none | *Throughput unit* (Basic, Standard), *processing unit* (Premium), *capacity unit* (Dedicated) — partitions organise, they are not the capacity unit | None. Google states Pub/Sub "does not have partitions" and "scales automatically based on demand" |
| **Coupling** | Apache Kafka itself; the exit is to another Kafka | Runs Kafka: AWS says MSK "runs open-source versions of Apache Kafka" | AWS-proprietary API — `PutRecord`, `PutRecords`, `GetRecords`, `SubscribeToShard` | Kafka's *protocol* over a non-Kafka implementation — "provides an Apache Kafka endpoint", "implements the same protocol" | Proprietary gRPC and REST API |
| **Throughput shape** | Whatever you provisioned, running whether or not load arrives | Provisioned capacity, or MSK Serverless, which AWS describes as "a throughput-based pricing model" | Provisioned by shard, or the on-demand family, which AWS describes as needing "no capacity planning" | Prepurchased capacity; Auto-inflate "automatically scales up by increasing the number of throughput units" — up only | Google states it "dynamically adjusts capacity for individual topics and subscriptions" |
| **Failure and recovery model** | Kafka's, and the tuning is yours — see the linked streaming file | Kafka's, since it is Kafka: ordering total within a topic-partition | Ordering scope is the shard, but the write guarantee is weaker than Kafka's — see below | Partition "can be thought of as a commit log"; partition count can rise on Premium and Dedicated but not fall, and raising it re-maps keys | At-least-once delivery; ordering is per *ordering key*, and Google's verb is "expected to be received in order" |
| **Ecosystem fit** | The whole Kafka ecosystem, and the whole operational burden with it | AWS states "existing applications, tooling, and plugins from partners and the Apache Kafka community are supported without requiring changes to application code" | AWS-native: Kinesis Client Library, enhanced fan-out | Microsoft describes "a multi-protocol event streaming engine that natively supports Apache Kafka, AMQP 1.0, and HTTPS" | Google's client libraries; the documented Kafka bridge is the Pub/Sub Group Kafka Connector |

### The coupling row is the one that decides

Three registers, not two, and flattening them is how this comparison gets written wrong:

1. **It is Kafka.** AWS's own words for MSK: it "runs open-source versions of Apache Kafka". Leaving means moving to another Kafka.
2. **It speaks the Kafka protocol without being Kafka.** Microsoft's words for Event Hubs sit consistently at this register — *endpoint*, *implements the same protocol*, *built-in Apache Kafka compatibility* — never "is Kafka". And Microsoft states the coverage is partial: the Kafka endpoint is supported on the standard, premium and dedicated tiers, excluding Basic; Kafka Streams and Kafka Transactions are documented as public preview in Premium and Dedicated; Kafka compression is documented for Premium and Dedicated only, with `gzip` as the supported algorithm; generated shared access signature tokens are unsupported there. Repeat Microsoft's migration claim only with their hedge: you can "often use an event hub's Kafka endpoint from your applications without any code changes" — *often*, and they say so.
3. **It is a proprietary API.** Kinesis Data Streams and Pub/Sub. Source it honestly: neither AWS nor Google publishes a sentence saying "this does not speak Kafka". What is documented is that the API is theirs — enough to reason with, and a different statement from a vendor denial.

The strongest vendor-side support for treating this axis as decisive comes from Google, arguing for its *other* product: "While Pub/Sub's autoscaling and global data distribution make it easier to operate, Apache Kafka APIs are much more broadly adopted. If you plan to use independent messaging systems in different on-premises or cloud-provider environments, Managed Service for Apache Kafka can give you a more consistent experience across your applications." That settles an error in both directions — "Google has no Kafka" is wrong, since *Managed Service for Apache Kafka* is a documented separate product (sized by aggregate cluster vCPU and RAM, not broker count); "Pub/Sub is Kafka-compatible" is wrong too, since the documented bridge is a Kafka Connect connector, not a protocol surface.

### Three things that do not survive the translation

- **Kinesis ordering needs its own sentence.** The *scope* is the shard, like Kafka's partition. The *write guarantee* is not Kafka's: AWS documents that strictly increasing sequence numbers for a partition key require the `SequenceNumberForOrdering` parameter on `PutRecord`, that this parameter is not part of a `PutRecords` call, and that `PutRecords` "attempts to process all records in the natural order of the request". Since `PutRecords` is the batch path you reach for under throughput, "ordering works like Kafka" does not hold.
- **Pub/Sub's ordering scope is a key, not a partition.** Messages sharing an ordering key "are expected to be received in order" — Google's verb, weaker than Kafka's "guarantees". Publishers must publish a key's messages from the same region, though subscribers may connect from anywhere; an empty ordering key means no ordering; and Google documents a cost for turning it on, that ordered delivery "decreases publish availability and increases end-to-end message delivery latency". The upside is real: Google notes "ordering keys are expected to have a much higher cardinality than partitions", so the parallelism ceiling is not a partition count. A different abstraction, not simply a weaker one.
- **Event Hubs partitions are not capacity.** Microsoft separates them explicitly — capacity is the throughput/processing/capacity unit; the partition is "a data organization mechanism that enables parallel publishing and consumption". Sizing a namespace as though partitions bought throughput, the way shards do in Kinesis, is the standard Azure mis-sizing.

## The same six questions, asked of managed Spark

Compute is the top rung, so this costs less deliberation than the streaming decision — but the axes are identical, and two naming facts will date you. Verified 2026-08-08.

**Names first, because they moved.** Google renamed this product twice inside a year: the current name is **Managed Service for Apache Spark**, with two deployments the vendor calls "on clusters" and "serverless", and the product banner calls it "the new name for the product formerly known as 'Dataproc on Compute Engine' (cluster deployment) and 'Google Cloud Serverless for Apache Spark' (serverless deployment)". The `dataproc` prefix survives in image versions, cluster properties and resource names, so old material reads as current when it is not. On AWS, EMR Serverless is documented as "a deployment option for Amazon EMR" rather than a second service, and Glue's Spark offering is "AWS Glue for Spark". Databricks gives its Spark compute no separate brand — the documented split is serverless compute, classic compute and SQL warehouses. Fabric's Spark offering is a workload, not a product name.

**And Azure Synapse Analytics is not deprecated.** As of 2026-08-08, Microsoft's documentation describes it in the present tense and publishes no retirement date for the service. What is published is narrower and should be repeated narrowly: for *new* work the recommendation is to start in Fabric ("If you're new to data warehousing, start with Fabric Data Warehouse"); migration assistance exists and part of it is in public preview; and Fabric's own comparison page still sends you the other way for specific capabilities — "Use Azure Synapse Spark when you need GPU-accelerated pools, external Hive Metastore, or JDBC connections" — listing those same capabilities under Fabric's key limitations. Individual Synapse components *have* been retired, with explicit dates and unambiguous wording; the service as a whole has not. Both errors cost you: writing Synapse off strands a workload Microsoft still points at it, and calling the two equivalent ignores the stated direction for new build. This changes by announcement, not by technical evolution, so carry the verification date with it.

On the axes, the pattern repeats. Scaling granularity is named differently everywhere: workers in EMR Serverless, DPUs and M-DPUs behind Glue's worker types, DBUs as Databricks' consumption unit, capacity units and Spark vCores in Fabric. Coupling runs lower than in the streaming table for anything running open-source Spark against files in an open table format — the job stays portable even when the platform is not — and highest where the platform's runtime, catalog and notebook surface become what your team writes against. Job-level tuning is not a procurement decision at all: shuffle, skew, join strategy, caching and memory are [`spark-data-engineering`](../../spark-data-engineering/SKILL.md), and no service choice repairs a job wrong on those.

## Spark on a container orchestrator

Teams arrive at this framed as an exception, usually worded *should we run Spark on Kubernetes instead of buying any of the above?* It is not an exception: the same compute decision, on the same top rung, answered with the same six questions — and the answers usually run: operational burden high and owned by your platform team, not a vendor; scaling granularity fine; coupling low, the honest attraction; failure and recovery model yours to build and debug; ecosystem fit dependent on a platform capability you may lack.

Two boundaries, then this file stops.

**A stateful data store on a container orchestrator inherits every problem in the frame file, amplified.** The orchestrator's design centre is that a workload is replaceable — reschedule it, replace the node, let the controller converge. That is the cattle assumption in [`statefulness-and-the-one-way-door.md`](statefulness-and-the-one-way-door.md) built into a control loop, and the resources holding your history are the ones it was not written for. Running stateless compute there is a platform choice; running the store of record there is a reversibility decision deserving the bottom-rung review budget.

**Container orchestration itself is platform engineering, and outside this skill.** Scheduling, networking, autoscaling policy, cluster upgrades and the operator ecosystem are a discipline with its own depth, and a data-engineering skill teaching them badly would be worse than one that declines. This skill's contribution is the frame you carry into that conversation, not the conversation itself.

## Common mistakes

| Mistake | Why it hurts | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Reading "serverless" as "billed per request" | False as a rule: Amazon documents Redshift Serverless as billing capacity per unit of time, in RPU hours on a per-second basis, and Google defines BigQuery's use of the word by what you do not provision, under two pricing models | Ignore the label and read the meter — bytes scanned, capacity held over time, requests, throughput delivered — then decide on throughput shape |
| Naming a scaling unit without naming the mode that exposes it | The unit belongs to the mode or tier: MSK exposes broker nodes in Provisioned and none in Serverless, Kinesis exposes shards in provisioned mode only, Event Hubs uses three units across four tiers | State unit and mode together, always, and check the tier before quoting a unit |
| Treating Event Hubs partitions as capacity, the way Kinesis shards are | Microsoft separates them explicitly — capacity is the throughput, processing or capacity unit; the partition organises and parallelises — so partition maths buys no throughput in Azure | Size the namespace by its pricing unit and choose partitions for parallelism and ordering |
| "Kinesis orders like Kafka because both order per shard/partition" | The scope matches; the write guarantee does not. Strictly increasing sequence numbers need `SequenceNumberForOrdering` on `PutRecord`, absent from `PutRecords` — the batch call you use for throughput, which only "attempts" the request's order | Design for the documented guarantee of the API you actually call, and make consumers tolerate what it does not promise |
| Calling Event Hubs "managed Kafka" | Microsoft's register is *endpoint* and *implements the same protocol*, never *is Kafka*, and coverage is documented as partial — Basic excluded, Streams and Transactions in public preview on Premium and Dedicated, compression limited, SAS tokens unsupported there | Say "Kafka protocol surface", and check the specific feature you depend on against the tier you are buying |
| Writing off Azure Synapse Analytics as deprecated | As of 2026-08-08 Microsoft publishes no retirement date for the service, describes it in the present tense, and Fabric's own documentation still directs GPU pools, external Hive Metastore and JDBC connections to Synapse Spark | Say what is documented: Fabric is the recommended start for new work, individual Synapse components have been retired with explicit dates, the service has not — and date-stamp it |
| Using "Dataproc" as a current product name | Google renamed the product twice inside a year; the current name is Managed Service for Apache Spark with "on clusters" and "serverless" deployments, and the `dataproc` prefix survives only in image versions, properties and resource names | Use the current name, cite the vendor's "formerly" formula to help readers recognise old material — verified 2026-08-08 |
| Comparing services on features and settling coupling last | Coupling is the only one of the six axes reaching the bottom of the reversibility ladder; the other five describe things you can change later | Decide coupling first and treat the other five as tie-breakers within the coupling class you chose |
