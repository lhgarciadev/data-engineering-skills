# Star Schema and Grain

Dimensional modeling starts from a question OLTP schema design never has to ask: what does one row of this table *mean*? Get that answer wrong — the grain — and every join, every `SUM`, every dashboard number built on top inherits the mistake silently. Everything below builds toward that one decision.

## OLTP vs. OLAP: the normalization trade-off

Operational (OLTP) and analytical (OLAP) systems are optimized for opposite jobs, and Kimball states the split in his own words: "Users of an operational system turn the wheels of the organization. They take orders, sign up new customers, monitor the status of operational activities, and log complaints. The operational systems are optimized to process transactions quickly... Users of a DW/BI system, on the other hand, watch the wheels of the organization turn to evaluate performance... These systems are optimized for high-performance queries" (Kimball & Ross, *The Data Warehouse Toolkit*, 3rd ed., Ch. 1, p. 2).

OLTP schemas normalize to protect write integrity — every fact stored exactly once, so an update can't leave two copies out of sync. The formal levels of that normalization are relational theory, not Kimball's own contribution: E. F. Codd defined them. First normal form requires atomic column values with no repeating groups, traced to Codd's foundational 1970 paper, *"A Relational Model of Data for Large Shared Data Banks."* Second and third normal form get precise, citable definitions in his 1971 report: "A relation R is in second normal form if it is in first normal form and every non-prime attribute of R is fully dependent on each candidate key of R," and "A relation R is in third normal form if it is in second normal form and every non-prime attribute of R is non-transitively dependent on each candidate key of R" (E. F. Codd, *"Further Normalization of the Data Base Relational Model,"* IBM Research Report RJ909, 1971, §2.5 and §3.3).

Dimensional models deliberately trade that write-integrity guarantee for read performance — and Kimball frames it as a trade, not a shortcut: "You should resist the perhaps habitual urge to normalize data... Instead of third normal form, dimension tables typically are highly denormalized with flattened many-to-one relationships within a single dimension table" (same book, Ch. 1, p. 15). Worth a note of precision: Kimball Group's own material sometimes uses "second/third normal form" loosely when describing the resulting shape of a dimensional model, and one of their own 2003 articles admits as much, calling the fact-table/dimension-table split "confusingly referred to as denormalized." Treat Codd as the source for the formal 1NF/2NF/3NF definitions, and Kimball only as the source for *why* analytics denormalizes — not as a second authority on what the normal forms formally mean.

## Fact tables vs. dimension tables

A fact table holds the numeric measures of a business event, plus the foreign keys that connect it to its context: "A fact table contains the numeric measures produced by an operational measurement event in the real world... In addition to numeric measures, a fact table always contains foreign keys for each of its associated dimensions" (Ch. 2, pp. 41-42). Shape-wise, fact tables run in one direction — many rows, few columns: "Fact tables tend to be deep in terms of the number of rows, but narrow in terms of the number of columns" (Ch. 1, p. 12) — deep but narrow, not the "long and narrow" phrasing that circulates informally; the concept (rows grow continuously as new events occur) is the same either way. Fact tables in production commonly reach billions of rows; Kimball's own text treats that as routine rather than exceptional, at one point dismissing an alternative approach as "an impractical exercise with a billion-row fact table" (Ch. 1, p. 11).

Dimension tables carry the descriptive context a fact table doesn't repeat on every row: "The dimension tables contain the textual context associated with a business process measurement event. They describe the 'who, what, where, when, how, and why' associated with the event" (Ch. 1, p. 13). Structurally they run the opposite direction from facts — wide and shallow: "Dimension tables are usually wide, flat denormalized tables with many low-cardinality text attributes" (Ch. 2, p. 46), with far fewer rows than the fact tables they describe. That size asymmetry — a `customer` dimension with thousands of rows sitting next to a `sales` fact table with billions — is what makes the rest of this file's design choices (broadcast joins, snowflake trade-offs) make sense.

## Star schema and snowflake schema

### The star schema: a halo of dimension tables

A star schema is one fact table connected directly to every dimension that gives its rows context. Kimball's own description is more vivid than the "one join away" shorthand that circulates in practitioner writing — the actual text pictures a halo, not a hop count: "Each business process is represented by a dimensional model that consists of a fact table containing the event's numeric measurements surrounded by a halo of dimension tables that contain the textual context that was true at the moment the event occurred. This characteristic star-like structure is often called a *star join*" (Ch. 1, p. 16). The practical consequence of that halo shape — each dimension reachable from the fact table by a single join, with no intermediate lookup tables in between — is what makes the schema fast, not a phrase Kimball wrote himself.

Two things make that shape fast in practice. First, joins stay shallow: no dimension requires a multi-hop path through other lookup tables to reach the fact table. Second, dimension tables are small enough — per the size asymmetry above — to be broadcast-joined rather than shuffled in a distributed engine; see [spark-data-engineering's joins-and-skew.md](../../spark-data-engineering/references/joins-and-skew.md) for the broadcast-join mechanics that make a dimension-to-fact join a local, in-memory lookup instead of a network-bound shuffle stage. The shape is also what makes a star schema intuitive for BI tools and business users: one fact table in the middle, its context one join away in every direction, mirroring how an analyst already thinks about "sales, by customer, by product, by date."

### Snowflake schema: a hardening stance

Snowflaking normalizes a dimension's internal hierarchy — splitting, say, a product dimension's brand attribute into its own brand lookup table — the exact move OLTP design habitually makes and dimensional design deliberately resists. Kimball's stated reason is not that storage is now cheap; it's that dimensions are a rounding error next to fact-table size, so normalizing them barely moves total database size: "Because dimension tables typically are geometrically smaller than fact tables, improving storage efficiency by normalizing or snowflaking has virtually no impact on the overall database size. You should almost always trade off dimension table space for simplicity and accessibility" (Ch. 1, p. 15). The proportional-size argument, not a storage-cost argument, is the one to teach.

Kimball's own guidance on snowflaking hardened over time rather than staying fixed. In the 1997 *Dimensional Modeling Manifesto*, the stance was conditional: "I think that a designer can snowflake with a clear conscience if this technique improves user understandability and improves overall performance." By the 2013 third edition of the textbook, the conditionality is gone — the guidance is a flat recommendation to avoid it: "You should avoid snowflakes because it is difficult for business users to understand and navigate snowflakes. They can also negatively impact query performance" (Ch. 2, p. 50). Present this as a stance that hardened across 16 years of practice, not as a rule that was always absolute.

## The four-step Kimball design process

Every dimensional model gets built through the same four decisions, in the same order: "The four key decisions made during the design of a dimensional model include: 1. Select the business process. 2. Declare the grain. 3. Identify the dimensions. 4. Identify the facts" (Ch. 2, p. 38).

1. **Select the business process** — the operational activity being measured (an order being placed, a shipment being sent), not a department or a report.
2. **Declare the grain** — exactly what one fact-table row represents, before anything else is decided (see below — this step is load-bearing enough to earn its own section).
3. **Identify the dimensions** — the "who, what, where, when, how, why" that gives each fact row context, all of it consistent with the grain just declared.
4. **Identify the facts** — the numeric measures the business process produces, again all consistent with that same grain.

Grain comes second, before dimensions or facts, because every candidate dimension and every candidate fact has to be checked against it — you can't identify what belongs on a row until you know what the row represents.

## Grain: declare it at the most atomic level

Grain is the row's contract: "Declaring the grain is the pivotal step in a dimensional design. The grain establishes exactly what a single fact table row represents. The grain declaration becomes a binding contract on the design" (Ch. 2, p. 39). Kimball's guidance is to declare it as atomically as the source data allows — one row per line item on an order, not one row per order, not one row per day: "We strongly encourage you to start by focusing on atomic-grained data because it withstands the assault of unpredictable user queries; rolled-up summary grains are important for performance tuning, but they pre-suppose the business's common questions" (same page).

The reason is a one-way street: atomic data aggregates upward on demand, but an aggregate can never be un-aggregated back to the detail it discarded. Kimball Group's own Design Tip #92 states this plainly: "Aggregated data... can easily be derived from the atomic data, but necessarily must truncate or delete most of the dimensions... Aggregated data is NOT the place to start a design!" Declare the grain at the lowest level the business process actually produces, and every future unpredictable question — the one nobody thought to ask at design time — still has the detail it needs to answer.

Getting the grain wrong or leaving it ambiguous has a direct, mechanical cost: it's the same cardinality-inflation bug covered from the SQL side in [sql-data-engineering's joins.md](../../sql-data-engineering/references/joins.md), in its fan-out (cardinality inflation) section. A join against a dimension or fact table that isn't unique at the grain you assumed silently multiplies rows before any `SUM` or `COUNT` runs — the grain declaration is what should have caught the mismatch before the query was ever written, not after a dashboard number came out wrong.

## Additivity: how facts aggregate

Not every numeric measure sums the same way, and getting this wrong produces a technically-correct-looking query with a meaningless total. Kimball's own three-way split: "The most flexible and useful facts are *fully additive*; additive measures can be summed across any of the dimensions associated with the fact table. *Semi-additive* measures can be summed across some dimensions, but not all; balance amounts are common semi-additive facts because they are additive across all dimensions except time. Finally, some measures are completely *non-additive*, such as ratios" (Ch. 2, p. 42).

- **Additive** — sums correctly across every dimension (order line amount, units sold). The default, and the easiest to reason about.
- **Semi-additive** — sums correctly across every dimension except time; an account balance is the canonical example. Summing a balance across accounts on a given day is meaningful; summing it across days is not — it double-counts the same money. Kimball's own text names the required workaround plainly: "Semi-additive facts, such as account balances, cannot be summed across the time dimension... You are forced to use counts and averages" (Ch. 1, p. 11). "Last value as of a point in time" is a widely-taught alternative for the same problem — reporting the balance as of period-end rather than averaging it — but that specific technique doesn't have a verbatim Kimball citation; treat it as standard industry practice alongside counts/averages, not as a Kimball quote.
- **Non-additive** — never sums, at any grain (unit price, any ratio or percentage). Kimball's own example is unit prices: they "can never be added" (Ch. 1, p. 11). The fix is to store the fully additive components underneath the ratio (e.g., total revenue and total units, rather than the average unit price itself) and recompute the ratio from those components after aggregating, not to aggregate the ratio directly.

## The three fact table types

Kimball names exactly three, and one of the three breaks the "fact tables are insert-only" rule that otherwise holds throughout dimensional modeling.

- **Transaction fact table** — one row per measurement event: "A row in a transaction fact table corresponds to a measurement event at a point in space and time" (Ch. 2, p. 43). An order line, a page view, a payment. Rows are inserted once and never revisited.
- **Periodic snapshot fact table** — one row per entity per fixed period, dense even when nothing happened: "A row in a periodic snapshot fact table summarizes many measurement events occurring over a standard period, such as a day, a week, or a month... these fact tables are uniformly dense in their foreign keys because even if no activity takes place during the period, a row is typically inserted... containing a zero or null for each fact" (Ch. 2, p. 43). A daily account-balance snapshot is the textbook pairing with the semi-additive balance example above.
- **Accumulating snapshot fact table** — one row per process instance (an order, a claim), revisited and updated as the process passes each milestone: "There is a date foreign key in the fact table for each critical milestone in the process... As pipeline progress occurs, the accumulating fact table row is revisited and updated. This consistent updating of accumulating snapshot fact rows is unique among the three types of fact tables" (Ch. 2, p. 44). An order-fulfillment row that fills in `ordered_date`, then `shipped_date`, then `delivered_date` as each milestone happens is exactly this pattern — and the only fact-table type in this list where an `UPDATE` after the initial insert is the expected, designed-for behavior rather than a modeling mistake.

## Common mistakes

| Mistake | Why it hurts | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Quoting Kimball as saying a dimension is "one join away" from the fact table | Not his actual phrasing — the book's own image is a fact table "surrounded by a halo of dimension tables" (the star join) | Use "halo of dimension tables" / "star join" as the citable phrase; "one join away" is fine as your own explanatory shorthand, not a quote |
| Justifying "avoid snowflaking" with a storage-cost argument ("disk is cheap now") | Not Kimball's actual reasoning, and it invites the counter-argument once storage gets expensive again | Cite the real argument: dimensions are geometrically smaller than facts, so normalizing them barely changes total database size |
| Attributing 1NF/2NF/3NF definitions to Kimball | They're Codd's relational theory (1970/1971), not a Kimball contribution | Cite Codd for the formal normal-form definitions; cite Kimball only for the OLTP-vs-OLAP motivation for denormalizing |
| Writing "grow without bound" or "long and narrow" as a Kimball quote | His own phrasing is "deep... but narrow" | Use "deep but narrow" when quoting; the continuous-growth concept can still be taught, just not in quotation marks |
| Declaring grain after picking dimensions and facts, or leaving it implicit | Every candidate dimension/fact has to be checked against the grain — deciding it last (or never) is how fan-out and mismatched joins get shipped to production | Declare the grain first, in one sentence, before naming a single dimension or fact |
| Starting a design from pre-aggregated data "for performance" | Aggregation is a one-way street — rolled-up data can't recover the dimensions it truncated | Start at the most atomic grain the business process produces; aggregate later, on top of that detail |
| Summing a semi-additive or non-additive fact across the wrong dimension | Summing an account balance across time, or averaging a unit price directly, produces a number that looks valid but means nothing | Use counts/averages (or last-value-as-of-a-date, as standard practice) for semi-additive facts across time; recompute non-additive facts from their additive components |
| Treating an accumulating snapshot row as immutable, like a transaction fact | Its entire purpose is to be revisited and updated as the process passes each milestone | Model milestone columns explicitly, and treat in-place updates as expected for this fact table type only |
